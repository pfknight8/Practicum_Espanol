# Participles and Gerunds

These are the "non-finite" forms of verbs, meaning that they do not correspond to a person, number, or tense. Instead, these are often used in conjuction with auxiliary verbs to form compound, 'perfect' tenses, or to convert the verb into a noun or adjectival form.

## El Gerundio / Los Participios Presentes

Translating to the *gerund*, these are roughly equivalent to the English "-ing" form of a verb. In Spanish, the gerunds are, for all intents and purposes, the same as *present participles*--el participios presentes--with no practical distinction between these. This is in contrast to English, where gerunds are for creating the noun form of a verb, and present participle for adjectives, adverbs, or compound verbs.

In fact, from an English grammar perspective, it's acually more appropriate to think of these as present participles, as you will see in the use cases, below.

### Formation

First, it's worth going over how the gerund/present participle is formed.

- For -ar verbs: -ando
- For -er/-ir verbs: -iendo

Of course, there are some exceptions, or 'irregulars' to watch out for. Fortunately, these are fairly straightforward. The primary one is going to be -er/-ir verbs whose stems end in vowels--in these cases, the ending changes to **-yendo**, replacing the *"i"* with a *"y"* to break up the series of vowels.

The other irregulars are going to revolve around stem changing verbs, such as "decir -> diciendo" or "dormir -> durmiendo." In general, -ir verbs with a stem change in the present tense will also have a change here, but that vowel will change to a single "i" or "u," not the "ie" or "ue" where otherwise applicable. And note, -ar and -er verbs do not change--i.e. they keep their infinitive root--even if they experience a stem change in the present tense (such as pensar -> pensando).

### 1. Progressive Actions (AKA Progressive Tenses)

These are used to form progressive tenses--for Present, Imperfect, Preterite, and Future--as one may see referred to in their relevent files.

This is usually formed by taking the verb 'estar' + the present participle. Estar will take the tense conjugation, while the present participle will remain the same no matter which tense one is trying to use. For example, "I am eating" = "Estoy comiendo."

### 2. Simultaneous Actions

In describing two things that happened at the same time. This can be thought of as the "while ___ing" construction in English.

For example, "I studied *while listening* to music" = "Estudié *escuchando* música."

### 3. Manner/Method

In explaining *how* something is done. This can be thought of as the "by ___ing" construction in English.

For example, "I learned by practicing" = "Aprendí practicando."

### 4. Other Uses

Gerunds may also be used in a few other odd cases--these are generally considered grammatically incorrect, but may show up in colloquial usage, so it is good to be aware of them.

It is sometimes used to indicate the result/consequence of an action: this is know as the **"gerundio de posterioridad"** (gerund of posteriority), and likely arises from influence by English. It frequently appears in translations, news reports, and casual speech. For example, "He studied hard, *getting* a good grade." = "Estudió mucho, *sacando* una buena nota." Remember: this is, at least technically, grammatically incorrect, but you'd likely be able to get away with it in everyday speech.

Gerunds can be used to shorten sentences, as is done using points 2 and 3 above. But it is also sometimes used by omitting a subject or auxiliary verb. In a way, this involves creating a 'gerund phrase,' or seemingly a dependant clause; the key thing here is that one does not include a specific subject or preposition--those would indicate using an infinitive. These generally are used when expressing (i) cause or reason, (ii) condition (if), or (iii) the use cases above--ie time (while or during) or means/methods.

For example,  "Not having money, I decided to stay home." = "No teniendo dinero, decidí quedarme en casa." instead of "Como no tenía dinero, decidí quedarme en casa."

The last point where you may find them is in **fixed phrases**, such as idioms and expressions, where they may not otherwise seem to fit.

### 5. Notable differences from English

As I mentioned above, these are actually not really used as gerunds in the English sense. In fact, that usage is actually handled by a verb's infinitive form.

For example, "*Swimming* is fun!" = "¡*Nadar* es divertido!" Or "I do not like *running*." = "No me gusta *correr*."

In these examples, the infinitive form is used where English speakers would pull out a gerund. This is something to be mindful of, and a great example of how direct translations won't always work out.

There is another case where we use gerunds in English where they do not in Spanish, and this involves the use as an adjective. In Spanish, gerunds cannot be used as an adjective, and instead must be rephrased or replaced with a relative clause.

For example, "the *crying* baby" = "el bebé *que llora*," or literally "the baby *that cries*."

## El Participo

The Spanish Past Participle form of the verb is used when converting the verb to an adjective, or to create perfect tenses. They function similarly to the English past participle, essentially being the "-ed" or "-en" form of the verb, such as in "provoked," "closed," "woven," or "spoken."
